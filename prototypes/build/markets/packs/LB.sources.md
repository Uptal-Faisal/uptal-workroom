## Payments
- **Context (high confidence).** Since the 2019 banking crisis, Lebanese bank deposits are split in two. Pre-crisis dollar deposits ("lollar") are restricted. "Fresh" dollar accounts and cards were exempted from the restrictions under BDL Basic Circular 150 (2020). Old cards are often blocked or limited for foreign and online use. Fresh-dollar Visa and Mastercard cards, and prepaid cards from banks and non-bank issuers, are what Lebanese people use to pay international subscriptions such as streaming and software.
- **Checkout.** `base.json` prices LB in USD. That fits the market: USD is the de facto pricing currency, and fresh-dollar cards are charged in USD with no FX step. So no local acquirer is assumed, unlike TN and LY.
- **Card first, `brands: ["visa","mc"]` (high confidence).** Visa and Mastercard dominate. Amex is not a mainstream Lebanese card, so it is left out. The card sub-line names fresh-dollar and prepaid cards because Lebanese users know old bank cards may fail.
- **No PayPal tile (fact-check change, medium-high confidence).** The first draft had PayPal as the second tile because it assumed Lebanese send-only accounts exist. The fact-checker's knowledge is the opposite: Lebanon is one of the markets PayPal does not serve (with Pakistan, Iraq, Syria, Libya and others), and Lebanese residents get around this with accounts opened abroad. A PayPal tile would mislead local users, so it was removed, the same choice as DZ, LY, IQ, JO and EG. Users with a foreign PayPal account almost always also hold the foreign card behind it, and the card tile covers that card. The fact-check could not confirm this live either: web search was used up and the proxy blocked paypal.com. **[verify in PayPal's country list (paypal.com/worldwide); if Lebanon is listed and its accounts can approve billing agreements, restore the tile after the card]**
- **Whish Money not a tile (conservative).** Whish is the leading Lebanese wallet and money-transfer fintech, and it issues prepaid Visa cards (medium confidence). Those cards are covered by the card tile. I could not confirm whether Whish offers a merchant checkout that can charge a recurring subscription for a foreign merchant. **[verify; if it does, add Whish as the second tile, after the card]** OMT is in the same position.
- **Wallets `[]` (medium confidence).** To my knowledge, neither Apple Pay nor Google Pay has officially launched with Lebanese issuers. Some users have them through foreign cards, but that is not standard here. **[verify on Apple's "Apple Pay participating countries" page and Google Wallet's country list; if Apple Pay has launched with Lebanese banks, add "apple"]**
- **BNPL `[]` (high confidence).** Tabby, Tamara and Klarna do not operate in Lebanon, and local banks offer no consumer BNPL that can pay a subscription.
- **`localCardBrand: null` (high confidence).** Lebanon has no domestic card scheme comparable to mada, KNET or Meeza. Local cards are Visa or Mastercard.

## Tax
- **VAT 11% (medium-high confidence).** This has been the standard rate since VAT was introduced in 2002 (Law 379/2001). Successive budgets raised fees and other taxes, and VAT increases were floated in several of them, but as far as I know the standard rate is still 11%. **[verify: 2026 budget law; PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, Lebanon, "Other taxes"]** Fact-check note: the checker vaguely recalls reports from early 2026 that the cabinet approved a 1-point VAT rise to 12%, together with a fuel levy, to pay for public-sector salary increases. A rate change needs a law passed by Parliament, and the checker does not know whether one passed. The rate stays at 11% until this is confirmed, but check it first: if a law raised it, set `tax.rate` to 12.
- **`incl: true`, label "VAT included" / "شامل الضريبة على القيمة المضافة" / "TVA incluse".** Lebanese consumer pricing is expected to show the final price, so the conservative display is a VAT-inclusive price. "الضريبة على القيمة المضافة" is the official Lebanese term (Ministry of Finance usage).
- **Open question [verify].** I could not confirm whether Lebanon requires non-resident sellers of B2C digital services to register for VAT. If it does not, Uptal may not owe Lebanese VAT at all. The label stays true either way as long as Uptal absorbs any VAT, so it is a safe default.

## Work rights
- **Legal basis (high confidence).** Decree No. 17561 of 18 September 1964 regulates foreigners' work. A foreigner needs Ministry of Labour pre-approval (موافقة مسبقة), then a work permit (إجازة عمل) from the Ministry of Labour and a work residence from General Security (الأمن العام). The employer applies. The permit is tied to one employer and one job, so changing jobs needs a new permit. "إجازة عمل" is the Lebanese official term and is used in the Arabic copy.
- **No free-movement bloc (high confidence).** No other citizenship is exempt, so `autoFor: ["LB"]` only. Lebanon allows dual nationality, so the citizen sub-line says "including dual nationals".
- **Option "Family of a Lebanese citizen" (medium confidence).** General Security grants a free "courtesy residence" (إقامة مجاملة) to foreign spouses and children of Lebanese citizens. That includes the husband and children of a Lebanese woman, who cannot pass on her nationality. As I understand it, holders may work but still need a Ministry of Labour work permit, so the sub-line says the employer still applies. A foreign wife of a Lebanese man can also claim nationality after one year of marriage (Nationality Decree 15/1925, art. 5), and then she is a citizen. **[verify current General Security rules]**
- **Option "Palestinian refugee registered in Lebanon" (high confidence on the law, medium on practice).** Law 129/2010 amended Labour Code art. 59. It exempts Palestinian refugees registered with the Interior Ministry from the reciprocity condition and from the work-permit fee. Law 128/2010 gave them NSSF end-of-service benefits. A permit is still required, and many professions stay closed to them. These include the syndicated professions (medicine, law, engineering and others) and the Ministry's reserved list. This is a large, real status, so it gets its own option with neutral wording.
- **Option "Foreign national with a Lebanese work permit".** The sub-line warns that a new job needs a new permit. Lebanon has no "transferable" permit like the UAE.
- **Last option: needs a work permit.** The employer must seek pre-approval and justify the hire. The sub-line warns that many finance roles are reserved.
- **Syrian nationals (medium-high confidence, not in the copy).** Ministry of Labour decisions since about 2014-2015 limit Syrian workers to agriculture, construction and environment/cleaning. They can therefore rarely get permits for finance roles. I left this out of the user-facing copy because it is politically sensitive and changes often. The matching rule covers it: if you need a permit, we skip reserved roles.
- **Matching rule.** Foreigners who need a permit are steered to employers that actually hire foreign staff: UN agencies (UN-ESCWA has its HQ in Beirut), NGOs, embassies and multinationals with regional roles. Jobs reserved for Lebanese are skipped. This is the conservative reading of the reserved-professions list, which covers most accounting and banking roles.

## Nationalisation ("Professions reserved for Lebanese", المهن المحصورة باللبنانيين)
- **Mechanism (high confidence).** The Minister of Labour issues decisions listing the professions and trades reserved for Lebanese (المهن والحرف المحصورة باللبنانيين فقط), updated every year or every few years. The list consistently includes "administrative, banking, insurance and educational work of all kinds", naming roles such as director, manager, accountant, cashier, secretary, IT and sales/marketing roles. **[verify the number and date of the latest decision on labor.gov.lb before citing it anywhere; the copy does not cite a number]**
- **Exceptions (medium-low confidence).** Decisions have carved out exceptions for some groups, for example foreigners born to a Lebanese mother or married to a Lebanese, and Palestinians born in Lebanon. A December 2021 decision under Minister Bayram widened these exceptions, and the State Shura Council suspended it in early 2022. Because the exact current scope is uncertain, the copy only says "many jobs" and "usually", and does not promise any exemption.
- The desc also states that every foreign hire needs a permit and that employers must usually show the role cannot be filled locally (high confidence for the permit, medium for the "can't be filled locally" test, which is applied through the pre-approval review).

## Sensitive fields
- **Photo `optional` (medium confidence).** Photos are common on Lebanese CVs, especially at local firms and for client-facing roles. Multinationals, UN agencies and international NGOs do not expect them; UN forms do not use them. "Optional" is the conservative middle. JO chose `common`. Lebanon's employer mix (many multinationals, UN agencies and NGOs) is why I went one notch lower.
- **Gender `ask`, DOB `ask` (medium-high confidence).** Bayt profiles and Lebanese bank and corporate careers portals usually require gender and date of birth. DOB is part of the usual personal-details block on Lebanese CVs.
- **Nationality `ask` (high confidence).** Nationality drives the reserved-professions and work-permit logic.
- **Marital `none`.** It often appears on Lebanese CVs and on some forms, but it is not needed to apply and is a bias risk. Same as JO, TN, LY and AE.
- **Never religion or sect.** Some older Lebanese forms asked for sect (الطائفة). We never ask it.

## Legal
- **Privacy law (high confidence).** Law No. 81 of 10 October 2018 on Electronic Transactions and Personal Data (قانون المعاملات الإلكترونية والبيانات ذات الطابع الشخصي). Its personal-data part requires controllers to file a declaration with the Ministry of Economy and Trade, and some categories need a licence (medium confidence on the procedure). Lebanon has no independent data-protection authority, so the privacy line names the law only.
- **Renewal / withdrawal (medium confidence).** Consumer Protection Law No. 659 of 4 February 2005 has a chapter on distance and home contracts. It gives the consumer a 10-day right to withdraw, counted from the contract date for services or from delivery for goods. I am not certain of the exact article number (I believe art. 55) or of the exceptions, for example whether starting to use the service ends the right. **[verify with Lebanese counsel; if an exception applies, add "unless you have started using the service"]** Fact-check note: Law 659 is modelled on the French distance-selling rules, and the checker expects the next article (art. 56) to exclude services that have already started with the consumer's consent. If so, the current line promises more than the law requires. That is safe for the consumer but is a commercial commitment. The auto-renew line follows the same form as the other packs.

## Salary (`per: "month"`, `f: 0.09`)
- **Low-medium confidence; background knowledge only.** After the crisis, LBP salaries collapsed. Most private employers in finance now pay wholly or partly in fresh USD, and pay has partly recovered. My best estimate for a Senior Financial Analyst / FP&A with about 8 years, in Beirut, at a bank, telecom, large group or multinational, is about USD 1,800-3,000 gross a month. UN national-staff grades and regional-hub roles at Murex or Azadea pay more. Local banks often pay less than before the crisis.
- I chose USD 2,340, giving f = 2,340 / 26,000 = 0.09. Noura's 26,000 becomes about USD 2,340 now and her expected 34,000 becomes about USD 3,060. For comparison, JO uses 1,500 JOD, about USD 2,115. Lebanese senior finance pay at top employers is in a similar band.
- **[verify against Bayt salary pages, Hire Lebanon or regional recruiter surveys, and L'Orient Today labour-market reporting]**

- **`govPlatforms: []`.** Lebanese job seekers do not go through a government platform. The National Employment Office (المؤسسة الوطنية للاستخدام) exists but is marginal. Work permits are an employer-side Ministry of Labour and General Security process, not something the applicant uses.

- **Workweek `mon-fri` (high confidence)** for banks and most private employers. Some firms add a Saturday half-day.
- **`noticeDays: 60` (medium-high confidence on the law).** Labour Code art. 50 sets notice by length of service: 1 month under 3 years, 2 months for 3-6 years, 3 months for 6-12 years and 4 months for more than 12 years. A mid-career mover with 3-6 years at the current employer is the typical case, so 60 days. Notice can run longer if the contract says so.
